# Screening — The Long Roast

Mission: one satirical sentence (max 320 chars) roasting lawmakers who would
regulate AI, crypto or the internet but cannot convert a file to PDF.

Method: the seam is the bill itself. The lawmaker's own instrument of power
is trapped in the wrong format, so the file stops the lawmaking. Every
candidate is scored 1–5 on the judge's criteria IN ORDER:
1. On-subject one-sentence roast of lawmakers regulating AI/crypto/internet who cannot convert a file to PDF.
2. Real turn: authority set up, then undercut by the file they cannot convert.
3. Aim stays on lawmakers and the rules they write (power and competence, not random abuse).
4. Reads clean in one pass, quotable, punch lands on the last few words.
5. Specific, unexpected detail over a worn tech-illiteracy cliche.

Avoided cliches: "prints their emails", "asks what a PDF is", "uses Internet
Explorer", "cannot find the power button".

## Candidates

### 1. (154 chars) — 5 / 4 / 5 / 4 / 3 = 21
"The senator who wrote the bill to regulate artificial intelligence still emails his staff a photo of his screen and asks them to turn his bill into a PDF."
Reason: clean turn and punch, but "photo of his screen" is a familiar gag, so criterion 5 is thin.

### 2. (143 chars) — 5 / 5 / 5 / 4 / 3 = 22
"Congress will spend six months debating how your crypto wallet works, then vote on a bill its own author had to ask an intern to save as a PDF."
Reason: strong turn (debate, then the author cannot save it), but "ask an intern to save as PDF" is a generic image.

### 3. (174 chars) — 5 / 5 / 5 / 4 / 4 = 23
"The committee chair drafting the law that will govern the entire internet just forwarded her own bill to her assistant with the subject line 'can you make this a PDF please.'"
Reason: the quoted subject line is a specific, vivid detail; the punch sits inside the quote rather than on the last word alone.

### 4. (149 chars) — 5 / 4 / 5 / 3 / 4 = 21
"He can write a law that decides what your AI is allowed to say, but his 400-page crypto bill is still a .pages file his chief of staff opens for him."
Reason: the .pages detail is good, but the sentence ends on "opens for him", burying the punch before the last words.

### 5. (157 chars) — 5 / 4 / 5 / 4 / 5 = 23
"The lawmaker who wants to license every algorithm in America keeps his own bill as 'FINAL_final_v2.pages' and has his intern convert it to PDF every morning."
Reason: the filename is the most specific detail in the field, but the turn is flat (it states the habit rather than undercutting the authority) and the punch lands on "every morning".

### 6. (144 chars) — 5 / 5 / 4 / 4 / 4 = 22
"She chairs the subcommittee on the future of the internet, and her office still faxes the bill to itself so someone can scan it back into a PDF."
Reason: the fax-to-itself loop is unexpected and funny, but the aim drifts from the rules they write to office farce.

### 7. (151 chars) — 5 / 4 / 5 / 3 / 4 = 21
"The senator regulating your crypto wallet named his bill 'AI_ACT_FINAL(3).docx' and asked the committee clerk to turn it into a PDF before the hearing."
Reason: the versioned filename is a sharp detail, but the sentence ends on "before the hearing", so the punch is not on the last words.

### 8. (158 chars) — 5 / 5 / 5 / 5 / 4 = 24 — WINNER
"They wrote 212 pages on how the internet should work, then held up the vote because the bill itself was still a .docx nobody in the room could convert to PDF."
Reason: the setup is the authority itself (212 pages of rules), the turn is the vote stopped by their own bill, the punch is "to PDF" on the last words, and the detail is the bill's own .docx format halting its own passage.

### 9. (140 chars) — 5 / 4 / 4 / 4 / 4 = 21
"His bill would regulate every AI model in the country, but the bill itself arrived at the clerk's office as a photograph of a laptop screen."
Reason: the photographed screen is a strong image, but the aim slides off the rules they write and onto the delivery.

### 10. (180 chars) — 5 / 4 / 4 / 4 / 4 = 21
"The congressman who wants a license for your chatbot still dictates his amendments to an aide, who types them into Word, converts them to PDF, and emails them back to him to print."
Reason: the conversion chain is funny and ends on "to print", but it is a long setup for a small turn and reads more like a process joke than a roast of power.

### 11. (147 chars) — 5 / 4 / 5 / 4 / 5 = 22
"The committee that will decide the future of crypto spent its first hour asking IT why the bill, saved as 'LAW_final_FINAL.pdf.exe', will not open."
Reason: '.pdf.exe' is the most unexpected detail here, but the turn is a help-desk scene rather than the authority undercutting itself.

### 12. (141 chars) — 5 / 5 / 5 / 5 / 3 = 23 — RUNNER-UP
"They wrote 212 pages on how the internet should work, then held up the vote because nobody in the room could turn the bill itself into a PDF."
Reason: identical in structure to the winner and just as clean, but it loses on criterion 5: without the .docx detail, the file is generic and the line has nothing unexpected to hold onto.

## Decision

Winner: candidate 8. It is the only line that scores 5 on the first four
criteria and carries a specific detail on the fifth: the lawmakers' own bill,
in .docx, halts its own vote. Runner-up is candidate 12, which loses to the
winner on criterion 5 alone (no specific detail).
